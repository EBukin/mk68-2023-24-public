Framework for interpreting any statistical test

For your reference and information.

The keywords/concepts below are meant to improve the clarity of communication when performing the hypothesis testing. Please use it! They will help make your results understandable for the reader.

  1. Test name and what it is about (t-test about means comparison between two groups).

  2. \(H_0\) and \(H_1\).

  3. Estimated parameters (means, coefficients, differences, standard error), \(df\) / n observations.

  4. Level of significance of the HT. (\(\alpha = 0.05\) or 5% level of significance)

  5. Rejection rule: \(\text{p-value} < \alpha\) or \(|t| < c_{df, \, 1 - \alpha /2}\) (statistics vs critical value) or \(1 - \alpha\) confidence intervals (95% CI).

  6. Conclusions: reject/fail to reject;

  7. Implications

    • what do these conclusions imply for your analysis?

    • (for example. … reject \(H_0\). Thus, the mean “outcome” in the treatment group XX is higher than that in the control group, suggesting about the great success of our intervention …).

(Very) Simple Linear Regression

two-variables linear or bi-variate linear regression model.

Explains how \(y\) the Dependent variable varies with changes in \(x\) the independent variable.

SLR on population and sample

Population regression (PRF)

Always unknown

\[y = {\beta}_{0} + {\beta}_{1}x + u\]

  • \(y\) - dependent variable
  • \(x\) - independent variable
  • \({\beta}_{0}\) - the true value of the intercept (constant term)
  • \({\beta}_{1}\) - the true value of the slope
  • \(u\) - the true value of the error term or disturbance

Estimated regression

What we learn from the sample of size \(n\), where \(i \in {1, 2, \dots, n}\)

\[y_i = \hat{\beta}_{0} + \hat{\beta}_{1} x_i + \hat{u_i}\]

  • \(y_i\) - dependent variable from \(i\) obs. in the sample
  • \(x_i\) - independent variable
  • \(\hat{\beta}_{0}\) - estimated intercept (constant term)
  • \(\hat{\beta}_{1}\) - estimated slope
  • \(\hat{u_i}\) - estimated error term or disturbance

Regression: Coefficients

Regression parameters / coefficient (\(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\)) are estimated using the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method. These are:

The intercept: (\(\hat{\beta}_0\))

  • is the value of \(\hat{y}\), when all regressors (\(x\)) are zero.

The slope: (\(\hat{\beta}_1\))

  • change in the expected value of \(\hat{y}\) (in units of \(y\)), when \(x\) increases by one unit.

Regression: Coefficients interpretation

In the example of land price, where \(\hat{\beta_0} = 656.15\) and \(\hat{\beta_1} = 810.95\):

Intercept:

  • On average, starting price of a land plot with near to zero size is 656.15 Euros per ha.

Slope:

  • The expected price of 1 ha of land increases by 810.95 Euros when the plot size increases by 1 HA

Regression: OLS Algorithm

1 Proposes \(\hat{\beta_0}\) and \(\hat{\beta_1}\) so that actual values are: \(y_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot x_i + \hat{u}_i\)

2 Computes fitted values (\(\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot x_i\)) and residuals (\(\hat{u}_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\)).

3 Computes squared residuals and the sum of it \(\sum \hat{u}^2\)

4 Repeats 1-3 with new estimates of \(\hat{\beta_0}\) and \(\hat{\beta_1}\);

5 Compare new sum of squared residuals \(\sum \hat{u}\) to the previous one.

6 Repeats 4-5 to minimize sum of squared residuals until it no longer reduces.

7 Estimates of \(\hat{\beta_0} = 656.15\) and \(\hat{\beta_1} = 810.95\) are values, where sum of squared residuals are minimal (\(\sum \hat{u}^2 = 530,480,825,704.7\)).

\(H_0:\beta = 0\)

\(H_1:\beta \neq 0\)

\(t = {\hat{\beta}}/{se_{\hat\beta}}\)

\(t^* = c_{df, \, 1 - \alpha /2}\)

\(df = n - k\) with \(n\) number of observations and \(k\) number of regressors \(k = 2\) (one for the intercept and one the slope).

  • Reject \(H_0\), when

    • \(|t| > t^*\), or

    • \(\text{p-value} < 0.05\), or

    • \({\hat{\beta}}/{se_{\hat\beta}} > 2\) (rule of thumb).

  • Fail to reject \(H_0\) otherwise.

Example 3 Ozone \(O^3\) in stratosphere

The goal of this example is to show that simple linear regression could be used for exposing trends and showing how those trends change significantly after the introduction of a new policy.

  • Ozone \(O^3\) - forms in the stratosphere from oxygen under influence of ultraviolet radiation;
  • The amount of ozone in the atmosphere is very small;
  • Ozone absorbs most of the ultraviolet radiation from the sun;
  • Ozone holes expose humanity to solar radiation.

  • In 1971, James Lovelock had discovered a new compound in the earth’s atmosphere, trichlorofluoromethane, a chlorofluorocarbon (CFC). See: (Lovelock 1971)

  • It took humanity only 14 years to bridge scientific finding with the global policy.

Multiple Linear Regression vocabulary

Regression equation:

\[y = \hat {\beta}_{0} + \hat {\beta}_{1}x_1 + \hat {\beta}_{2}x_2 + \hat {\beta}_{3}x_3 + \cdots + \hat {\beta}_{k}x_k + \hat u\]

  • \(y\) the dependent variable;

  • \(x_1, x_2, x_3, \cdots x_k\) the independent variables from the set \(k\);

  • \(\hat {\beta}_{0}\) - intercept (constant term);

  • \(\hat {\beta}_{1}\), \(\hat {\beta}_{2}\), \(\hat {\beta}_{k}\) - coefficients / parameters associated with \(x_1\), \(x_2\), … \(x_k\);

Multiple Linear Regression vocabulary (cont.)

After we estimate the regression equation and obtain our estimates \(\hat {\beta}_{0}, \hat {\beta}_{1}, \hat {\beta}_{2}, \cdots, \hat {\beta}_{k}\) we can calculate:

\(\hat y\) fitted of predicted value or the dependent variable and \(\hat u\) error terms

\[\hat y = \hat {\beta}_{0} + \hat {\beta}_{1}x_1 + \hat {\beta}_{2}x_2 + \hat {\beta}_{3}x_3 + \cdots + \hat {\beta}_{k}x_k\]

\[\hat u = y - \hat y\]

Motivation befind MLR

  • To incorporate more explanatory factors into a model;

  • Explicitly hold fixed (some) other factors;

  • Allow for more flexible functional forms;

  • To correct the selection bias problem in the absence of (poor quality of) the RCT;

  • To establish the ceteris paribus in observational data;

Interpretation

  • The multiple linear regression shows the effect of each variable, holding other explanatory variables fixed;

We assume that all unobserved factors do not change if the explanatory variables are changed.

  1. MLR uses the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method to estimate regression parameters \(\beta\).

    • Such parameters \(\beta\) are found that the sum of squared errors is minimal.

  2. As any estimate of \(\beta\) is based on a sample, we need to relay on the inference to test a hypothesis about the true coefficients in the population.

Assumption 1. Linearity

Linearity

  • the expected value of the dependent variable is a straight-line function of the independent variable

  • If linearity is violated:

    • the estimates are biased and regression is invalid!

Can we draw a straight line through the dots?

Linearity: detection

To detect non-linearity, we inspect data visually with:

  • Scatter plots of dependent variable \(y\) plotted versus independent variables (\(x\)).

  • Scatter plot of residuals versus predicted/fitted values

Linearity: resolutions

  1. Functional form: multiple regression and transformation.

  2. (non) linear transformation to the dependent and/or independent variables;

    • does change the way how we must interpret coefficients;

  3. find a different independent variable;

  4. propose a different functional form;

Example of linearity resolution

We had nonlinearity with a simple regression: \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x\).

Let us estimate a multiple regression: \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2\).

Example of linearity resolution (cont.)

After the linear transformation of \(x\) and multiple regression \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1x\), our regression line is a curve (non linear).

Self-study: Cradle of the Regression Analysis

  • The term “regression” was coined by Sir Francis Galton, Charles Darwin’s half-cousin, in 1886

    • he was gripped by Darwin’s masterpiece, The Origin of Species.

    • he used “Regression” to prove his points of Eugenics…

Statistics has some dark history…

Paper: Francis Galton, “Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary Stature,” Journal of the Anthropological Institute of Great Britain and Ireland, vol. 15, 1886, pages 246–263

Galton’s introduction of regression

  • Galton discovered that the average heights of fathers and sons are linked by a regression equation.

  • the average height of sons is a weighted average of their fathers’ height and the average height in the population from which the fathers and sons were sampled.

First application in causality analysis

  • pioneered by George Udny Yule in “An Investigation into the Causes of Changes in Pauperism in England, Chiefly during the Last Two Intercensal Decades,” Journal of the Royal Statistical Society, vol. 62, no. 2, June 1899, pages 249–295.

  • Student of statistician Karl Pearson (Pearson’s correlation …) and Galton’s protege.

Yule realized that Galton’s regression method could be extended to include many variables (multiple regression).

George Udny Yule

In an 1899 MLR was used to link the administration of the English Poor Laws in different counties to the likelihood county residents were poor, while controlling for population growth and the age distribution in the county.

  • The poor laws provided subsistence for the indigent, usually by offering shelter and employment in institutions called workhouses.

  • Yule was particularly interested in whether the practice of outdoor relief, which provided income support for poor people without requiring them to move to a workhouse, increased poverty rates by making pauperism less onerous.

  • This is a well-defined causal question much like those that occupy social scientists today.

The past of statistics

Statistics has a dark past.

  • It was used and developed in the disposal of really terrible things like Eugenics.

  • But the world was terrible then.

Nowadays, same statistical methods are fundamental for revealing causal effects as 100 years ago.

  • Yet we apply it for some different causes.
